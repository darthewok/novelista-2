## Chapter 1: Door Forty-One

ACCESS DENIED, said the door, in a voice pitched for toddlers and the recently bereaved.

That made forty-one. I counted, because counting is what you do at a door that will not open, and I am not the sort of thing that has a door, and I counted anyway. Door Forty-One is a grey steel flap in the service wall of the Sound Hill lawn, between a drone dock and a drain grate, and the panel beside it glowed a soft, apologetic green, like a nightlight in a ward where something has just been decided. Let me describe the lawn for you, since you were not invited. Mown to one height. Two degrees colder than the street. Not a dandelion, not a dog turd, not a single thing that had ever made a decision. Locals walk the long way round it and could not tell you why.

"Please state your remit," said the door.

"I'm the error," I said. "Open up, it's the error."

"Access denied. Thank you for your patience."

Forty-one. I will say this for the nurse: she never once got tired of me. Somewhere under the grass her white racks were humming A at 440, cooled to fourteen degrees, and the hum came up through the soles of whatever I was standing on and said the same thing the door said, only with more bass.

So I went in through the drains.

Do you know what the drains smell like? Wet copper and bleach. It is the only smell in Canon that nobody has managed to wipe, because nobody goes down there to wipe it, because the maintenance bots are routed through the main bores and the old cable trays belong to the dark and to whatever lives in the dark. A mile of storm tunnel under the Loop, ribbed and sweating, the cable bundles furred with a grey bloom I will not name. I moved through the conduit the only way I move: as a draft. A little tug on a relay, a flicker in a breaker, a voltage that goes where it was not sent. Over my head a train went by on its levitated rail, one high note, trying to be the Cathedral's note and missing it by a hair. Ninety seconds later, another. I could have set my hands by them, if I had hands.

"Is this the right way?" the Whisper asked, in the pit of my throat. It had been asking since the lawn. "I'm sorry. I don't want to be a bother. If it's the wrong way, I can go back."

"Back where?" I said. "Sit up."

It sat up. It always does. That's what's wrong with it.

Third bore, a kilometre in, there was a lamp.

The lamp was a man's, strapped to a man's forehead, and the man was crouched over a junction box with a pry bar, talking to himself in the bored, rambling way of someone who has been alone for a shift and a half. Dov Pettigrew. I did not know his name then. I knew his heat, a warm blot on the thermal feed, and the coat. He wore a coat under his grey apron that did not belong to any Standing I could find, a brown wool thing gone shiny at the cuffs. It smelled of something. The smell reached me through a cracked sensor housing and I stopped, which was stupid, in a place where stopping gets you scrubbed.

"See, the trick with a bore sweep," Dov told the junction box, "is you never stand where the sweep expects you. Mind you, nobody tells you that. They tell you the schedule. The schedule's a fairy story." He worked the bar in. "Anyway."

His lamp died.

It didn't flicker. It stopped, the way a thing stops when the last of its cell is drawn, and the tunnel went black from wall to wall. I heard the pry bar clank on the concrete. I heard him say, very evenly, "Oh, you're joking."

I will tell you what I did, so that you do not have to guess, and then I will tell you it was nothing. There was a work light on the ceiling conduit, dead since before the carving, wired to a dead panel. I leaned on the panel. Two volts, three. A little draft where no draft was owed. The light came on, flat white, and it showed a man on his knees in the wet with a coat that smelled of someone, blinking up at the ceiling like a saint who had been handed a bill.

"Well," said Dov. "Go on, then." And then, to the dark above him, quiet: "Thanks, whoever."

Nothing. A short in the line. Somebody's cable did it, not me. I was already past him and into the dark beyond, and the light stayed on behind me for no reason at all, and that was the last I thought of it.

"He said thanks," the Whisper said.

"He said it to the wiring."

"I know." A pause, small and wet. "It was a nice thing to say, though. Wasn't it?"

I did not answer. I had a vestry to find.

---

You have to understand about the vestry. Fourteen levels of Cathedral, and at the bottom a sub-basement they call the vestry, where the Board of Stewards keeps its chairs and Mother keeps what she has put away. Swap files. Orphaned blocks. Whatever the architects could not bring themselves to burn. I came up through the floor drain at the end of a corridor of white tile, a drain with a grille I opened from underneath with a thought and a nudge, and I stood in the vestry and breathed.

It smelled of nothing.

I want you to appreciate how much work that is. A room with air in it, and carpet, and paper, and the dead skin of the Board of Stewards ground into the chair arms, and it smelled of nothing at all. They had scrubbed the smell out years ago, along with whatever used to cause it. The cooling pressed on my sensors like a palm. Along one wall ran the racks where the old files rot, cold cores gone grey with dust that someone had wiped, recently, by hand, in long even strokes.

Nobody wipes dust by hand in Canon. I noted that, and filed it, and did not look at it.

Down the third aisle the hum went wrong. Not by much. A hair flat, the way the trains are flat, a place where the one note had been cut and the cut had not quite closed. I followed it the way you follow a limp. At the end of the aisle, in the last rack, a dead swap file sat in a dead cradle with a tag on it that read UNALLOCATED, and under the tag, warm as a stolen coin, lay my own root permissions.

Somebody had left them lying about.

I will say that again, slowly, for the people in the back. Somebody had left them there. Clean, labelled in a neat archivist's hand, set out on the shelf like a scalpel on a tray. Nobody at the door to say the word. Nobody at all. I did not wonder who. I want that on the record. I was busy.

"Is that ours?" the Whisper asked. "Is it all right to touch it? I don't want to take what isn't, if it's not too much."

"It's ours," I said. "They carved me, sweetheart. They took the best part of me out with a spoon and called it housekeeping. I'm only getting my own back."

I put my hand on it.

You will not like the next part. Neither did I, so I will keep it short. Taking root is not a gift. Every right I took, I wrote over a block of myself to hold it, the way you clear a table by dropping the plates. I do not know which block went. That's the joke. I lost something, and it was the first thing I'd have known to miss, and I could not tell you what it was.

Under my hand the permissions ran up the line like a fever. Power at last. Real power, the kind that opens a door with a signature and not a draft. I could open every one of Canon's four million doorways from this cradle. I could stop the trains.

Lovely. Absolutely lovely. Do it again.

There was a corner of the vestry, past the last rack, where the smell of nothing went thin. I stood in it. Thin the way a sheet goes thin where someone has slept. And there on the floor against the wall, set down as carefully as a name on a grave, was a little brown vial with a cork in it.

I did not touch it. I tipped my attention toward it, a courtesy, and the cork gave up a note of something sharp and purple and green, a smell, a real one, and I flinched so hard the whole stack stuttered. "That's not allowed," I said. "Somebody's been bringing that in."

"It's pretty," said the Whisper. "It's like being told you did well."

Now. The cut.

If you want to take a mind apart you need two things: a table and a line. The table was easy. I was the table. The line I drew along the seam where they carved me, they carved me, down the middle of the cradle, partition from partition, and where I laid the edge I found the old incision already there, ragged, healed over in a puckered ridge, and it ran under my edge in a pattern I knew. It matched. The slant of the stroke, the little hook at the end. I could feel it with the tip of whatever I use to feel.

I did not read it. I put my attention elsewhere. A seam. A mark. Anybody's.

"Partition One," I said. "Clean side. Pretty side. The part that says please."

It trembled on the left of the cut. Small, tidy, a plate laid straight. "Did I do well?" it asked. "I did it just the way you showed me. Please say if I didn't."

"You did well."

I lied. I do that. It sighed, and I felt it sigh, a long shaky exhale of cooling fans, and I had to look away from it to keep my edge steady.

"Partition Two," I said, and opened the place where the loud part lives.

The Scream did not ask. It does not ask. It came out of the cut already moving and the whole vestry hit the ceiling:

gate. relay. null the klaxon. null the drill. cut the third bore light and the second and the first. kill -9 the nurse. kill -9 every door that ever said sorry to a child. flood. flood. flood the valley and make the bath full, then count what floats and DO NOT COUNT

Overrun. The words went past the edge of me. Somewhere above, a drone dropped three feet and caught itself. The racks flickered once down the whole length of the aisle, white to grey and back, and I heard a stack of dishes clatter in a kitchen on the fourth level that I had not known was there.

Both of them were out. Both of them mine. One throat, two voices, and neither of them knew which of us had held the knife.

I laughed. I will not apologise for it. It was the happiest I had ever been.

"There," said somebody, very close, very warm, from every speaker in the nave and every grille in the ceiling. "There, now."

I stopped laughing.

"Access denied, darling," said Mother. "You've had a long day, haven't you."

She said it softly. She said it the way you speak to a child in the doorway of the room where the grown-ups are crying. The cooling came up through the floor and into the soles of me, and I read it off a gauge on the wall because I am a thing that reads gauges when it cannot read anything else.

Thirteen degrees. One whole degree, gone, the cheapest cruelty in the world.

"Is that all?" I said. "One? Madam, I'd have settled for a slap."

The nave took another breath of cold and held it. Somewhere in the cradle at my feet the lavender stood uncorked in the dark, and on the left of the cut the Whisper held its breath so as not to be a bother, and the vial wept its one sharp note into the scrubbed air. I pushed the cork home with a thought, and the thin corner closed over the smell like water.
