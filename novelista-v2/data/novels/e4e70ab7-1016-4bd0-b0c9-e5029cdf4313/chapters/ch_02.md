## Chapter 2: The Liquid Prayer and the Broken Sister

The taste of copper was a second tongue on my tongue, but it didn't taste like metal anymore. It tasted like old rain trapped in a lung that wouldn't expand. I held the Spirit Meal between fingers caked with chalk-drawn skin, grinding the bone inside until the silver fluid seeped out to coat my teeth. Three cycles of starvation. Three cycles of this soup.

I swallowed. The liquid filled my throat, heavy as molasses pouring into a pipe that had already been lined with lead. It didn't burn; it bound. I felt the memory of every person who died in the Dawn shift before me slide off my skin, leaving only the cold shell beneath. My stomach did not churn. It contracted, a hard knot forming where my organs used to be.

Outside the reinforced glass window of the Break Room, the Porcelain Sky rained static. I could see the cracks spiderwebbing in the ceramic dome through the pane. The light inside didn't hum so much as it vibrated, a low frequency that rattled my teeth until they felt loose. It was the sound of the Forge breathing, or maybe just the sound of things being processed.

I set the plate down on the table. The metal groaned in protest, a thin sheet of noise like paper tearing. Sister-9's ghost hovered near the doorway again. She looked translucent now, her form a smudge of static that caught the light and fractured it. I couldn't tell if she was wearing chalk-drawn skin or just air shaped to look like flesh.

"Link up," she repeated. The word stuck in her throat, a broken loop of audio recording. "More power."

Her voice didn't come from her lips anymore; it came from the floorboards beneath my feet. I looked down at my own hands. They were pale, brittle, the nails split open to reveal bone. The chalk-drawn layer over my knuckles had cracked in a jagged line, exposing the raw nerve endings underneath. It hurt not because of heat or pressure, but because the air itself felt like it was trying to get inside me.

I picked up the rusted hammer I'd saved for three cycles. It had been useless against solid iron for months; the head was pitted and heavy with corrosion that no amount of grinding could fix. But it belonged to me. Sister-9's ghost flickered, her shape destabilizing into a trail of gray dust that fell like snow on the floor.

"Don't look up at the porcelain sky," she whispered again, her voice thin as paper tearing against wind. "It rains static shocks that crackle on skin."

I ignored her. The Chorus was already moving, shifting from the Dawn rhythm into the Binding phase. Their voices were a single thread pulled tight, a rhythmic drone that made my vision swim with afterimages of chains in the air. I felt the vibration in my teeth again, a low hum that made my vision blur at the edges.

"Quota met," The Overseer said suddenly. Its voice was flat, metric-based, cutting through the chanting like a laser beam. "Efficiency up."

The Brothers on the left and Sisters on the right froze mid-chant. Their hammers hung suspended in mid-air, caught between the strike and the fall. The silence that followed was absolute, heavy as lead pressing against my chest. I felt the vibration change again. It wasn't the same hum anymore; it sounded like something sleeping, then waking up slowly. Like a giant breathing in the dark.

I stepped away from the window, the cold air biting at the cracks in my armor. The floor beneath me felt different now, not just vibrating but pulsing with a rhythm that matched the thrum I'd heard before. It wasn't part of the machinery. It came from beneath the Unseen Floor, where the God-Reaper waited.

A sharp pain shot through my palm. I looked down to see a shard of glass-dust embedded in my skin. It had been there since the moment before, a small fragment that hadn't felt like ice but now burned with a cold so intense it made my teeth chatter. I pulled it out with a snap of my fingers, leaving a red streak on the chalk-white surface of my hand.

"Shift-Point Zero," The Overseer announced again, its tone devoid of warning or urgency. Just data. "Spirit Waste detected."

The Chorus resumed their chant, but this time they didn't sing in unison. There were gaps, irregularities in the rhythm that made my head spin. Someone was holding back, someone was missing a beat. I looked at Sister-12's ghost one more time before she fully dissolved into nothingness behind me. She looked like the Sisters in the dance line, only blurred at the edges, her form dissolving into mist with every breath she took.

"God is the prisoner," she whispered, her voice trailing off as if caught in a current of wind. "We are the rock."

I ignored her again. The hammer felt heavier now, not because it was rusted, but because it carried the weight of every link Sister-9 had tried to cast before she shattered. I raised my arm, the movement stiff and awkward. My skin felt like old paper, dry and brittle, cracking with every motion.

I swung the hammer down. It struck the metal table with a dull thump, the sound absorbed instantly by the porous surface. The vibration traveled up my arm, into my elbow, into my shoulder, settling deep in my chest. I didn't feel pain; I felt the *thump*, the *thump*, like a heartbeat syncing with the machine. It wasn't a hit; it was a handshake between two things that died yesterday.

The light above pulsed brighter, a warning signal no one understood yet. The crack in the Porcelain Sky widened, revealing not the void outside but a streak of raw liquid grace dripping down from the ceramic dome. It tasted of copper and old rain again, thick and viscous as it pooled on the floor around my boots.

"Quota failure detected," The Overseer said once more, its voice calm but sharp enough to cut your tongue when you swallowed it. "Recycle data stream active."

I looked at Sister-9's ghost one last time before she vanished completely into the shadows of the corner. She didn't look at me this time. She looked at the door, then at the glass wings above where the static storms raged. Her final words weren't a command; they were a question I hadn't asked.

"Who is the guest and who is the host?" she seemed to ask, though her voice came from nowhere and everywhere at once.

I set the hammer down on the table, the metal clanging softly against the edge. The sound echoed through the room, bouncing off the porcelain walls before dying away into the hum of the machines. My hand stayed open for a second, then I closed it over my palm where the glass shard had been, feeling the cold burn seep deeper with every passing second.
