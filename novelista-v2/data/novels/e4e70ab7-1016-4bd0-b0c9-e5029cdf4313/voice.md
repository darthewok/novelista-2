# Voice

## Part 1 — Guardrails (permanent)
- Third-person limited past tense unless the POV directive says otherwise.
- Body-first emotion; no direct emotion labels in narration.
- No filler adverbs in dialogue tags. Prefer "said" over decorative tags.
- Short sentences can carry shock. Long sentences can carry grief or awe. Use both deliberately.
- Do not moralize. Do not explain the theme in narration.

## Part 2 — This novel's voice
Chosen register: <Lynette>

### Exemplar passage — 2 paragraphs taken/adapted from the chosen trial

The air inside the White Forge didn't smell of oil or rust; it tasted like cold prayer, sharp enough to cut your tongue when you swallowed it. I woke up gasping, my lungs full of smoke that felt lighter than a funeral shroud, as if I was carrying the weight of an unburied spirit. The light here wasn't sun at all, but a sterile glare from porcelain skylights that hummed with a low frequency, vibrating in my teeth.

"Shift-point zero," a voice called out, not from a speaker but from the air itself, crisp and clear against the din of the machines. "Gate locks in six seconds. Initiate binding protocol." The word *protocol* hung heavy in the humid heat, mocking the idea of salvation. A man to my left stumbled over his station, his eyes rolling back as the machinery hummed him into a cylinder of recycled data. He was gone before I could scream, his spirit reduced to nothing more than fuel for the next batch.

### Speech patterns per character — how each major character talks

**The Narrator (Lynette):** Her speech cuts through the noise like a sledgehammer. She uses short, punchy sentences when she's in danger or shock, but switches to long, rhythmic, breathless clauses when describing the chanting workers or the machinery. She frequently drops articles for emphasis ("the gate" becomes "gate") and uses sensory metaphors involving heat, taste, and physical weight rather than abstract concepts. When she's angry, she repeats words for effect, not as a glitch but as a tool to drown out the machines. She doesn't say "I was scared"; she describes the cold knot in her stomach or the way the floor felt underfoot.

**The Overseer:** Speaks only in clipped commands and metrics. No emotions, no pleasantries. Uses present tense for everything except when reporting past failures ("He broke. He fell."). His voice is a flat, recorded tone that cuts through dialogue tags instantly.

**The Brother (Recycled):** His dialogue is fragmented before he's processed. He speaks in fragments or questions that cut off mid-sentence as the machinery takes hold. By the time he becomes a cylinder of data, his last words are just repeated commands like "Link up" or "More power," stripped of context.

**The Master (Voice from the ceiling):** Never appears physically. His "voice" is heard through the hum of the machines itself. He speaks in long, flowing sentences that describe fate as inevitable and beautiful, using words like *grace*, *link*, and *design* repeatedly. When he speaks, the floor vibrates harder.

**The Worker Chorus:** A collective voice. No single person is identified; they speak together. Their sentences are simple, direct, and repeated ("Blessing is labor"). They never use contractions that don't end in 'd' or 't'. They interrupt the narrator's thoughts with their own rhythm.

### Prose style specifics — 3-5 concrete sentence-rhythm rules, plus 2 short anti-exemplar paragraphs of what this voice must NOT sound like

**Rules:**
1. **The Breath Rule:** If a paragraph ends with a period, the next paragraph must start with a verb, a sensory detail (sight, sound, taste), or a dialogue tag that interrupts the flow. No "Once upon a time" or "I walked into..." unless it's an action verb first ("I broke").
2. **The Concrete Filter:** Every abstract noun (fate, soul, system) must be immediately followed by a physical object or sensation (iron, heat, hammer, vibration). If you can't see, hear, taste, or feel it, cut the word.
3. **The Caribbean Cadence:** When describing pain or rage, use repetition of the root word in different forms but not as a list (e.g., "broken, broke, broken again" is okay if it feels like a chant; "He was sad and angry" is a fail). The rhythm should mimic the sound of hammers hitting metal.
4. **No Internal Monologues:** Do not have characters think about their feelings unless they are speaking them aloud or acting on them physically. If Lynette thinks she loves someone, show her touching their arm, not her thinking "I loved him."

**Anti-exemplars (what to avoid):**

*   *Lynette felt a wave of dread wash over her as she looked at the empty chair. She knew this place was terrible and full of ghosts.*
    *(Bad: tells emotion instead of showing physical reaction, uses cliché metaphors)*

*   *The atmosphere inside the White Forge was thick with an unspoken tension that everyone felt but couldn't explain.*
    *(Bad: "thick with" + abstract noun = forbidden tell; passive construction)*
