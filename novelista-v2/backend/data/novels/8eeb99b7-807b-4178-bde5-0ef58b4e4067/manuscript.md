# Mika and the Lost Moonberry


---

## Chapter 1: The Silence of the Silver Reed

Mika sat at the threshold of the round oak door, her spine pressed against the rough bark. She counted. *One.* The air smelled of damp earth and old leaves. *Two.* The silence was not empty; it was heavy, pressing against her quills like wet wool. *Three.* She exhaled. The meadow beyond was a black pool, deeper than the night sky.

Usually, the fireflies wove their gold thread through the dark, a living tapestry of light. Tonight, there was only the void. The tall reeds stood like gray bones in the wind. No hum. No pulse. Just the hollow ache of absence.

A scuttling sound broke the stillness. Bramble burst from the ferns, his whiskers twitching violently. He chewed a mint leaf so furiously it snapped between his teeth.

“Friend Mika,” he panted, dropping to her level. His eyes were wide, white disks in the gloom. “The Moonberry is gone.”

Mika’s claws dug into the dirt. “Gone?”

“Vanished. The silver reed is bare. The fireflies… they are forgetting, friend. They forget the melody, and then the dark takes the song forever. The silence is already eating the roots.”

Mika shrank back. The Wide Open stretched beyond the oak, a terrifying expanse of unknown grass. Her stomach turned over. “I cannot go there,” she whispered. “I am too small for the wide sky. I got lost once. I will get lost again.”

Bramble nudged a strip of birch bark toward her. It was a map, drawn in charcoal. “Follow the straight line, friend. Keep to the path. You can do this.”

Mika looked at the map. The lines were sharp, logical. But the grass outside the oak was wild, tangled. “It is too dark,” she said.

“Look,” Bramble insisted.

From the reeds, a single spark flickered. It was weak, a dying ember in a storm. It pulsed once, twice, then faded to gray. The firefly was dying. Not just resting. Forgetting.

Mika’s breath hitched. If the light went out, the song died with it. Her fear of the wide sky warred with the terror of the dark. The dark was quiet. The wide sky was loud with possibility. But the silence was already pressing against the oak’s roots, seeping under the door.

She stood. Her quills trembled. She stepped over the threshold.

The grass rose to meet her knees. Then her chest. It was a green wall, thick and suffocating. Mika pulled out Bramble’s map. The charcoal line was clear. *Go straight.*

She walked. The grass swayed, brushing her quills. She kept her eyes on the line. But the line vanished. The grass grew taller, swallowing the ground. She turned left. The line was gone. She turned right. Nothing but shadows.

Panic rose in her throat, sharp and cold. The ground felt like it was shifting beneath her paws. She spun around, disoriented. The map was useless. The grass was a maze. She backed away, her heart hammering against her ribs, until her back hit the oak. She slid down, trembling, the map crumpled in her paw.

“Friend?”

A low hum vibrated in the air. Old Fen hovered nearby, his light dim, a pale yellow ghost. He had been here long-ago, when the berry was lost before.

“The line is a lie, little one,” Fen hummed. “The grass moves. Follow the silver. The scent of the berry. It calls to those who listen.”

Mika sniffed. The air was thick with the smell of damp moss and rotting leaves. She closed her eyes, trying to catch a whiff of silver. She inhaled deeply. Nothing. The wind shifted, carrying the scent of wet stone from the opposite direction. She turned. The wind shifted again. She was walking in circles.

Dizziness spun her world. The silence grew louder, a roaring in her ears. She sat down in the dirt, her quills bristling in terror. The meadow felt like a mouth, waiting to swallow her whole. She was too small. Too broken. Too lost.

Fen’s light dimmed to a grey ember. “By-and-by,” he whispered, “the song will be gone.”

Mika closed her eyes. She stopped looking for the map. She stopped listening to the silence. She focused on her nose.

She remembered the one time she had been close to the Moonberry as a kit. It had been far away, but the scent had drifted to her. Sweet. Cold. Like rain on stone.

*Rain on stone.*

She inhaled. The air was wrong. But there, faint, buried under the rot, was a hint of cold. A drop of rain on stone.

Mika opened her eyes. She did not look at the grass. She did not look at the sky. She looked at her nose. She stepped forward, guided by the scent, by the sound of her own paws on the earth. Her quills settled, aligning with her movement.

She found the reed. It stood alone, twisted in a clump of thorny vines. The berry was hidden, dark and cold. She could not see it clearly, but she could feel its pulse against her nose. A slow, steady beat.

*One.* She hooked a claw into the vine.
*Two.* She pulled.
*Three.* The thorn tore her paw. She did not flinch.

She worked carefully, one vine at a time. Her paws were steady. The scent grew stronger, sharper, sweeter. With a final pull, the berry slipped free.

It glowed in her paw, a soft, silver light.

Mika carried it back. She did not run. She walked slowly, the light illuminating her path. As she approached the oak, the berry brightened. The fireflies stirred, their lights waking, humming a low, grateful note. The song returned to the meadow, weaving through the dark.

Mika did not look up at the sky. She looked at her paws. They were just right.
